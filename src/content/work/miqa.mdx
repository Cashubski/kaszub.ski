## The problem

Diffusion-weighted MRI of the prostate is sensitive to geometric distortion, and an acquisition that looks plausible can still be unreliable for reading. My MSc thesis at UCL asked two questions in sequence. Can a model detect distorted acquisitions automatically? And, more usefully for any real workflow, can that model tell when its own judgement should not be trusted, so that the uncertain cases go to a person instead?

The second question turned out to be the interesting one. The most accurate architecture I trained performed poorly at identifying its own mistakes.

## What I did

- Assembled the experimental pipeline in PyTorch on a retrospective dataset of 1,027 acquisitions from 627 patients (single centre, University College London Hospital, anonymised; the data cannot be shared).
- Enforced strict patient-level five-fold cross-validation, so no patient appears in both the training and the validation side of any fold.
- Benchmarked four backbones (ResNet-18, ResNet-50, DenseNet-121, EfficientNet-B0) against classical baselines, then trained a three-seed DenseNet-121 ensemble.
- Investigated why the most accurate architecture performed poorly at identifying its own mistakes, looking at ensemble homogeneity and correlated errors between members.
- Built a heterogeneous nine-model ensemble to get a more informative uncertainty signal, then a retrospective triage simulation that defers the least-certain cases.
- Distilled the nine-model ensemble into a single student model, reducing inference from nine forward passes to one.
- Reported with bootstrap confidence intervals and paired Wilcoxon tests, and inspected behaviour with Grad-CAM, calibration analysis and synthetic-distortion validation.

## The finding, and the mechanism behind it

The three-seed DenseNet-121 ensemble was the most accurate model, with a mean AUROC of 0.819. Its uncertainty was close to useless. Uncertainty-error AUROC asks whether the cases a model is unsure about are the cases it gets wrong; 0.50 is chance. The DenseNet-121 ensemble scored 0.52. ResNet-18, a much shallower and slightly less accurate network, scored 0.64 (Figure 2).

A deep ensemble turns disagreement between its members into uncertainty, so I measured how much the members disagree. DenseNet-121 models trained from different seeds make strongly correlated predictions, with a mean pairwise correlation of 0.74 against 0.68 for ResNet-18 and 0.64 for ResNet-50. Members that agree with one another, including when they are wrong, leave the ensemble with little disagreement to report.

## Decisions that mattered

<dl class="decisions">
  <div>
    <dt>Split by patient</dt>
    <dd>Several acquisitions share a patient. Splitting by acquisition would have leaked patient-specific appearance across folds and inflated every number that follows, so the five folds are drawn at patient level.</dd>
  </div>
  <div>
    <dt>Report the spread across folds</dt>
    <dd>The headline AUROC of 0.819 carries an SD of 0.049. Seed variance is a different quantity; the SD quoted here is across the five folds.</dd>
  </div>
  <div>
    <dt>Make the ensemble heterogeneous</dt>
    <dd>If same-architecture members agree too much, mix the architectures. A heterogeneous nine-model ensemble, three architectures with three seeds each, reached an uncertainty-error AUROC of 0.685 against 0.522 for the three-seed DenseNet-121 ensemble, which made deferral worth simulating (Figure 4).</dd>
  </div>
  <div>
    <dt>Make the expensive model cheap</dt>
    <dd>Nine models are a lot to run on every scan. Distilling the ensemble into one student reduces inference from nine forward passes to one.</dd>
  </div>
  <div>
    <dt>Test without patient data</dt>
    <dd>The public repository ships a CPU-only pytest suite that runs on synthetic NIfTI volumes, so the code can be checked anywhere without access to the dataset.</dd>
  </div>
</dl>

## Deferral, and a check against physics

An uncertainty signal earns its keep when it decides which cases a model should not answer. Selective prediction does that: rank the cases by uncertainty, defer the least certain, and measure accuracy on what is left. It is a tunable deferral mechanism, shown retrospectively. The coverage level is a dial for whoever runs it, and nothing here was run on live cases.

A classifier trained at one centre can learn a shortcut that has nothing to do with the artefact. To test that, I injected synthetic geometric distortion of increasing magnitude into undistorted test acquisitions and watched the output.

## Limitations and status

This is a retrospectively validated proof of concept. The dataset comes from a single centre, the triage figures come from a simulation on held-out retrospective data, and nothing here has been deployed or validated in a clinic. The fold-to-fold spread in every figure above is large, as it will be with 627 patients. The results are evidence that the approach is worth carrying forward. They are not a clinical claim.

## What I learned

<div class="offset">

<p class="pull">Accuracy and self-knowledge are different properties, and optimising the first can quietly damage the second.</p>

An ensemble’s disagreement is only useful as an uncertainty signal if its members disagree in informative ways, which is why I looked at homogeneity and correlated errors. I also learned to write a result down in its plainest form: spread across folds, splits at patient level, a simulation called a simulation.

</div>
